Introduction

An ordinary differential equation involves derivatives with respect to only one variable.

Note

A partial differential equation (PDE) is a differential equation containing multiple variables.

The Order of a PDE is the highest order of the derivatives contained in it. We will focus mostly on second order PDEs.

  • A first-order PDE typically tracks things like velocity.
  • A second-order PDE typically tracks things like acceleration or curvature.

Laplacian

Note

The Laplace Operator Δ\DeltaΔ is defined as the sum over all unmixed second partial derivatives of a function.

The Laplacian is the divergence of the gradient. It measures how much the value of a function at a specific point deviates from the average value of its immediate neighbours.

Note

A function uuu satisfying the Laplace Equation Δu=0\Delta u = 0Δu=0 is called harmonic.

Intuitively, the Laplacian behaves like the second order derivative in one dimension. If a point is higher than its neighbours, then the Laplacian will be negative (downward curvature) and vice versa.

Note

An equation of the form Δu=f\Delta u = fΔu=f is called Poisson Equation.

Boundary Conditions

A PDE on its own has infinitely many solutions. Boundary conditions are used to "pin down" unique solutions. Two of the most important boundary conditions are

Note

  • Dirichlet Boundary Conditions: Our solution shall coincide with a function on the boundary of the domain Ω\OmegaΩ, so u(x)=g(x)u(x) = g(x)u(x)=g(x) on the boundary ∂Ω\partial\Omega∂Ω.
  • Neumann Boundary Conditions: We fix the normal outward derivative (the slope pointing out of the domain) ∂u∂ν=g(x)\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu} = g(x)∂ν∂u​=g(x) for all x∈∂Ω.x\in\partial\Omega.x∈∂Ω.

Heat Equation

The Heat Equation is of the form ut=Δuu_t = \Delta uut​=Δu, where utu_tut​ is the rate of temperature change. The rate of temperature change will eventually be zero. Therefore, for large ttt the equation becomes the Laplace Equation.

If a point is hotter than its surroundings, meaning Δu(p)<0\Delta u(p) < 0Δu(p)<0, then heat will flow away from it.

Schwartz Space and Distributions

Classical derivatives require point-by-point differentiability. If a function has a sharp corner or a jump discontinuity, its classical derivative fails to exist at that point. To solve PDEs with realistic, rough behaviors, we must stop evaluating functions point-by-point and instead study how they behave on average when integrated against smooth test functions.

Test Functions and Schwartz Space

The Space of Test Functions Cc∞(Ω)C_c^\infty(\Omega)Cc∞​(Ω) consists of smooth (infinitely differentiable) functions with compact support.

Tip

Remember that compact support means that boundary terms vanish during integration by parts. Because the support of ϕ\phiϕ is fully contained within Ω\OmegaΩ, the function ϕ\phiϕ (and all its derivatives) are identically zero on and near the boundary ∂Ω\partial\Omega∂Ω. Thus, all boundary evaluations drop out completely.

The Schwartz Space S\mathcal{S}S consists of rapidly decreasing functions that decay to zero faster than any polynomial can grow. They have "almost compact support". A standard example is the Gaussian bell curve e−x2e^{-x^2}e−x2. Boundary term vanish here also.

Distributions

In physical reality we often find possible "rough" solutions to PDEs that are actually not differentiable in the classical sense. Instead of looking at a rough function uuu directly, we view it as a Distribution TuT_uTu​. A distribution is a continuous, linear functional that acts on a smooth test function ϕ\phiϕ and outputs a single real number.

Tempered Distributions

The dual space of the Schwartz space S\mathcal{S}S is denoted by S′\mathcal{S}'S′. An element of this dual space is called a tempered distribution.

Note

Because S\mathcal{S}S contains functions that do not have compact support, S′\mathcal{S}'S′ is a smaller (more restrictive) space of distributions than the general space D′(Rn)\mathcal{D}'(\mathbb{R}^n)D′(Rn) (which is the dual of Cc∞C_c^\inftyCc∞​).

Fourier Transform

The Fourier Transform maps a function from the spatial domain into the frequency domain. It decomposes a signal into its constituent pure tones. For solving PDEs, its core value lies in its ability to turn differentiation into multiplication.

Tip

ddxeax=aeax.\frac{d}{dx}e^{ax} = ae^{ax}. dxd​eax=aeax.

Qualitative Aspects

  • Smoothing vs. Decay: The smoothness of a function determines the decay rate of its transform at infinity. If fff is smooth, its Fourier transform f^\hat{f}f^​ drops off rapidly.   • Convolution Theorem: Convolution in space simplifies to point-wise multiplication in frequency: F(f∗g)=(2π)n2f^⋅g^\mathcal{F}(f * g) = (2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}} \hat{f} \cdot \hat{g}F(f∗g)=(2π)2n​f^​⋅g^​.

Fundamental Solutions and Green's Functions

Motivation

The Fourier Transform is powerful, but it only works when our domain is the entire, unbounded space Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn. To solve Laplace Equations on bounded domains, we calculate the response to a single, isolated point mass at the origin (−ΔΦ=δ-\Delta\Phi = \delta−ΔΦ=δ). The resulting spatial function is called the Fundamental Solution. This function becomes our new building block allowing us to construct solutions – so called Green's Functions.

Weak Solutions and Sobolev-Spaces

Trace Theorem

Note

The Trace Theorem solves the problem of not being able to evaluate an equivalence class of functions on the boundary of a domain in a well-defined manner.

Since smooth functions are dense in the Sobolev Space, we can approximate a Sobolev function by smooth functions. One can show that the LpL^pLp-norm of these functions on the boundary is bounded by the Sobolev-Norm. Therefore the sequence of smooth functions, restricted to the boundary (restriction is well-defined for smooth functions), is a Cauchy-Sequence which has a limit in the Banach-Space LpL^pLp on the boundary. We define TuT^uTu to be that limit.

Tip

We used the densitiy of smooth functions in Sobolev Spaces to find a well-defined restriction on the boundary.

Gagliardo-Nirenberg Inequality

The derivatives control the slopes of the function. By bounding the LpL^pLp norm of the gradient, we restrict how fast the function can grow or spike. This makes it "more integrable", i.e. an element of even higher order LqL^qLq space.

Note

A main application of this inequality is the ability to embed Sobolev functions in higher order LqL^qLq spaces.
